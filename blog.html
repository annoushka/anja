we all want certainty, happiness, and peace.
we want to know that things will be okay. we want difficult feelings to resolve and for happiness to become something reliable enough to hold on to.
but life is more diverse than that. it gives us excitement and exhaustion, love and resentment, beauty and loss, clarity and uncertainty. and your nervous system has the capacity to hold all kinds of emotions. perhaps wellbeing is the development of enough inner openness to meet each experience with congruence and love.
happiness exists when we are feeling it. i think being content means not holding yourself back from real happiness. for me, one of the clearest examples is a beach day. i love contemplating the water, the sky, the smells, and i feel happiest when i can jump into the sea. there is something immediate about that moment. the temperature of the water, the movement of the body, the sensation of entering a different space: i am not trying to remember happiness or predict whether it will last. i am simply experiencing it.
feelings and sensations may be among our most direct forms of contact with life. they are not always comfortable, and they do not always tell us exactly what to do. but they show us that we are here.
when i speak about needing a baseline of neutrality, i mean that neutrality creates space. it allows happiness to arise without immediately clinging to it, and it allows pain to exist without concluding that pain is the whole truth.

can i notice this feeling without immediately judging it?
acceptance also does not mean liking what has happened. in the context of grief, it can mean acknowledging the reality of the loss and beginning to live in a world that has changed.
when my mother died, i lost a part of myself. my life already seemed meaningless and misaligned. i was not in a place where i felt fully connected to myself or to the direction of my life. then everything seemed to move into another dimension.
i remember thinking: everyone has mothers. i do not anymore. it frightened me that there was no clear return to what i had considered normal.
i was standing in a large room with beautiful plants and lots of daylight, in front of a light-brown wooden coffin that i had chosen myself. in that moment, i understood that i could only accept the fact that we would never see each other again. there was no positive side that could make it bearable immediately. there was only the plain, irreversible reality of it.
grief processing is not linear. sometimes it feels less like moving through a process and more like learning the shape of a new world.
i always felt neglected and that my mother did not give me enough attention and care. after she died, i no longer had anyone to address that feeling to. this was one of the strange contradictions of grief. the relationship had contained love, but it had also contained disappointment. death did not simplify it. it removed the possibility of asking for something different.
we often want to make the dead into uncomplicated figures: entirely good, entirely loving, entirely wise. but real relationships are not uncomplicated. we can love someone and still have been hurt by them. we can feel tenderness beside detachment, gratitude beside resentment.
none of these feelings cancels the others. maybe they are there waiting to get noticed and overtime recycled into something new like everything in the universe.
a full human experience does not require us to edit our relationships into a version that feels more comfortable. it asks us to make room for the whole truth.
i had lived with fear since childhood. it began with the precise moment when i understood that my mother was often sick and older than other mothers. i became afraid of her death. it was my biggest fear, and for many years it poisoned everything around me. every day, there was some version of the question: what if she does not come back from work? what if the painful expression on her face means that she is dying today?
this fear lived in my head for around twenty years. my mother was physically present, but in some way i was already anticipating her absence. she was half-dead to me—not because i loved her less, but because it felt like permanent uncertainty.
when she died, the fear had reached its feared conclusion. it did not mean that i stopped grieving, but the particular fear that had followed me since childhood was dead too. and it felt liberating.
after her death, many unexpected thoughts arose. i wondered about the thirty seconds before and after death. i wondered whether her brain had replayed memories from her life. i wondered whether she was happy when she saw them, or whether she felt satisfied with what she had achieved.
i do not know what my mother experienced. i do not know whether she was frightened, peaceful, aware, or somewhere beyond any category i can imagine. perhaps this is another part of being human: wanting certainty at the exact point where certainty is impossible.
wacky thoughts are still part of life. back then some were philosophical. some were frightening. some were oddly practical. some felt almost absurd. and i think this is normal because when something fundamental changes, the mind tries to orient itself. it asks questions, revisits memories, creates possibilities, and searches for a way to live beside what has happened.
not every thought needs to be solved. some thoughts can simply be noticed: this is what my mind is producing today. this is what grief feels like in my body. this is the question i cannot answer. this is the happiness i can still feel. that last statement matters. grief did not make happiness impossible. it made happiness more visibly temporary, and perhaps more precious.
a full life includes feelings we welcome and feelings we want to resist. it includes joy, fear, grief, desire, confusion, love, anger, wonder, and calm. it includes the ability to be deeply moved by a beautiful day and deeply changed by a painful loss.
no matter what is happening we get to choose to feel what is here. to let happiness be real when it arrives. to meet pain without judgment. to remain open to the fullness of experience, even when it does not resolve into certainty.

if you’re experiencing overwhelming or persistent grief please seek support from a qualified mental-health professional



imagine pulling back from your life until anything you’ve ever known shrinks to a whisper in a universe so vast it’s almost unimaginable. zoom out far enough and all of human history fits into a single breath of the universe. your entire life, with all its complexity and intensity, starts to feel like a flicker. still, in that flicker, you can open a book and let another "flicker” expand what you call “self” into something slightly larger than your personal history alone could make it.

reading is one of the few activities that reliably expands who we are without requiring us to go through experiences. it gives us new words, new beliefs, new permission slips, and new perspectives — anything that may blossom into new parts of our inner landscape.

one cannot pour their experience into another individual. each person discovers reality in their own way, through their own nervous system, history, and meaning-making.

if direct transfer of experience is impossible, what’s left?
inspiration and familiarity.
books can:
- make certain ideas, feelings, and ways of seeing the world feel familiar
- offer language for experiences the other person has already had but couldn’t name
- model a way of being that quietly says “this is also possible.”

this blog post is about reading but i am encouraging everyone to write as well because every human has a unique experience. everyone has a story to share. telling it in a book is both a challenge and an experiment.

writers attempt to compress years of living, feeling, and thinking into sequences of words and build entire universes using only language, rhythm, and imagery. that deserves respect. it takes courage to expose your inner world in a form that can be judged, misunderstood, or ignored.

while you’re reading this if something resonates and feels insightful it adds more possible paths to your internal map and that’s the real power of sharing. and you can meet any writing with curiosity, reflection, and willingness to be changed.

it’s astonishing that humans remain enthusiastic about playing with words when, on the surface, it seems every sentence has already been written somewhere, by someone.
but:
- no one has your exact combination of experiences, so no one can arrange words in precisely your way
- the same idea, in a different voice, can land for the first time in someone’s heart
- context changes meaning: a sentence read at 16, 30, or 50 is not the same sentence.
the miracle isn’t that words are new; it’s that they keep finding new homes.

on the receiving end, we as readers enrich our lives with:
- new words: more precise language for emotions, states, and needs. this alone can reduce inner chaos and increase self-clarity
- new beliefs: exposure to different worldviews challenges rigid assumptions and creates cognitive flexibility
- new permission slips: seeing others live, love, fail, and change gives implicit permission to do the same
- new perspectives: stories from other cultures, times, and identities evoke compassion and broaden the lens through which we interpret our own lives.

over time, these small shifts compound. reading doesn’t just add information; it changes the operating system behind how you think, feel, and choose.



we don’t just eat to fuel our bodies. we eat to celebrate, to connect, to remember who we are. food is woven into culture, family rituals, and personal identity — so building a healthy relationship with food is essential. and beyond the personal, how we eat ripples outward: our food choices affect the environment and contribute to climate change, which means our plates are also a place where values meet action.

when you cook at home, you naturally shift toward better ingredients, simpler methods, and more presence. you don’t need preservatives because you’re not optimizing for a warehouse, you’re optimizing for a human. that alone makes home-cooked meals generally more wholesome than processed options
there’s also a subtle, often overlooked benefit: exposure to diverse ingredients while cooking can support your skin microbiome. variety in what you touch, smell, and handle in the kitchen gently diversifies the microbial environment you live in — another reason real food isn’t just about what goes in your mouth
and then there’s the energy you bring to the meal. when you cook with care, that intention amplifies at the table. sitting down with family or friends, sharing a meal, slows the nervous system and strengthens connection. that’s not just cozy — it’s regulatory

food is more than just a source of nutrients — it's honoring pleasure and celebration and choosing in a way that aligns with your values.



i read this line in "the body keeps the score": “cultivating sensory awareness”

it’s more than a phrase—it’s a practice. you want to be as present as possible at every moment. it’s the opposite of being dysregulated. when we get traumatized we see things in a distorted way and give meaning to things that they don’t carry which creates disconnect from our own truth. the good news? regulation and presence feed each other. as you become more regulated, you become more present—and as you become more present, you become more regulated

sensory awareness is about accessing the autonomic nervous system through breath, movement, touch, and sensation

grounding exercises like “name five things you see, four you can touch…” are useful, but lasting regulation comes from weaving sensory pleasure into daily life:

- a plant you can touch and feel its living texture under your fingers
- a blanket that’s the perfect weight, warmth (and looks good)
- music that makes you move without thinking
- textures, scents, and sights that evoke an authentic, unforced response

these aren’t indulgences. they’re recalibration tools. when you give your body aesthetically and sensorily pleasing inputs, you un-bias it away from threat and toward safety. you’re not just relaxing, you’re teaching your nervous system a new baseline and as your sensory awareness deepens, you become less reactive and more resourced.

YOUR LIFE TIMELINE: CRAFTING YOUR COHERENT STORY

this powerful exercise helps you weave meaningful events, triumphs, challenges, and turning points of your life into a unified autobiographical narrative
by anchoring key moments to five-year intervals, you’ll get more clarity about your journey and identify what shapes who you are today

how to do it:
write down 2-5 pivotal events for each 5-year block (0-5 years, 6-10 years, 11-15 years etc)
helpful questions: what happened that felt truly significant (for instance, a move, achievement, loss, or realization)?
how did it impact your emotions, relationships, or direction?
what strength or lesson emerged?

connect the dots: look for recurring themes like resilience, growth, or shifts in values. reflect: what surprises you? how does this story inspire your next chapter? how can you progress it to what you want to be remembered for?
